# Personal Strategy

Read this reference when the decision owner is an individual and the question concerns life direction, career, identity, a portfolio of commitments, or a major personal allocation choice.

## Translation from organizational strategy

Preserve strategic rigor while removing unnecessary competitive language:

| Organizational term | Personal-strategy meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Winning aspiration | One meaningful, recognizable state of success for the current horizon |
| Where to play | One primary arena or target, with explicit boundaries and supporting contexts |
| How to win | A theory of distinctive value creation and sustainable thriving |
| Capabilities | Skills, health, relationships, reputation, capital, judgment, and optionality |
| Management systems | Resource allocations, boundaries, commitments, and decision rules required by the strategy |

Do not assume that success means maximizing income, status, productivity, visibility, or conventional career progression. Elicit the user's definition of a good life and treat it as a choice that may itself need examination, not as a value to impose.

## Focus discipline

Select one current arena, contribution, or compounding asset to receive disproportionate commitment. Other valued domains may be constraints, maintenance requirements, supporting capabilities, or explicit exclusions, but cannot remain coequal strategic priorities in the same strategy.

The personal Strategic Focus must identify what will receive more time, attention, money, relationship capital, or identity commitment—and what attractive alternative will receive less. Health, relationships, meaning, and security may be non-negotiable constraints rather than competing focuses. If the user is making genuinely separate decisions, scope and analyze them separately.

## Diagnose before prescribing

Look for the causal structure behind the request:

- Which commitments currently consume time, attention, health, money, and identity?
- Where does actual resource allocation contradict stated priorities?
- Which constraint is structural rather than a motivation problem?
- Which capabilities, relationships, reputation, or ownership assets could compound?
- What is being preserved by avoiding the choice?
- Which attractive options are mutually incompatible?
- Is the apparent problem a lack of direction, a transition, an execution failure, or an unwillingness to make a trade-off?

Select at most three questions that are most likely to change the strategy. Do not conduct a fixed life-domain inventory.

## Develop alternatives

Build options around meaningfully different commitment structures that could each become the one current focus. Include continuation of the current path as a real option. Useful differences may include:

- Deepening an existing arena versus entering a new one
- Concentrating on one compounding asset versus maintaining a portfolio
- Seeking control versus preserving flexibility
- Building a platform or ownership position versus selling time or expertise
- Making a staged experiment versus an irreversible commitment

For every option, expose what the person must stop, defer, or allow to decay. A personal strategy that leaves every door open is usually not a strategy.

## Evaluate personal options

Use the universal criteria, with additional attention to:

- Integrity with the user's stated values
- Energy and health sustainability
- Relationships affected by the commitment
- Learning, reputation, and ownership that can compound
- Option value and reversibility
- Financial and emotional survival floors
- Regret caused by action versus inaction

Do not reduce the choice to an optimization formula when values are incommensurable. Name the genuine tension and make the recommendation conditional on the user's stated priorities.

## Personal choice cascade

Express the cascade concretely:

1. **Meaningful success:** What one state is worth organizing the current horizon around?
2. **Chosen arena:** Which primary role, problem, community, or commitment receives disproportionate attention, and what lies outside it?
3. **Distinctive contribution:** What will the person do unusually well or differently, and for whom?
4. **Required capabilities:** Which few assets must be built, protected, borrowed, or acquired?
5. **Enabling commitments:** What resource allocations, boundaries, and decision rules make the choices real?

Keep enabling commitments strategic. Do not design morning routines, recurring reviews, productivity systems, or detailed task plans unless the user separately asks for them.

## Boundaries

- Do not diagnose mental-health conditions or substitute strategy work for professional care.
- Do not infer a personal profile from a single example.
- Do not impose fixed domains such as career, family, health, wealth, or spirituality; use only those relevant to the user.
- Do not treat an inspirational purpose statement as sufficient without allocation choices and trade-offs.
- Do not confuse a five-year prediction with a strategy under uncertainty.
